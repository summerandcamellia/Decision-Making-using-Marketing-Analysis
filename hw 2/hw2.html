Context

You work in the international division of a film studio. The studio will make 10 films next year. Your head of international has asked you to recommend which genres those 10 films should be.

She has told you three things:

  1. Most of the studio’s money now comes from outside the United States. A film that does well at home and badly abroad is a weaker film for this studio than its domestic numbers suggest.
  2. The slate is fixed at 10 films. Every slot you give to one genre is a slot you take from another. “Make more films that travel well” is not a recommendation.
  3. She will ask which numbers you used. A film that has not been made yet has no audience rating and no vote count. She will not approve a slate built on numbers that only exist after release.

You will use box office data for films released between 2000 and 2024. The data tells you what each film earned at home and abroad, how it was rated, what language it was made in, and its genres. It does not tell you what any film cost to make or to market.

This homework gives you the regressions to run. What it does not give you is the judgment: which model to trust, which films to include, and what to recommend. That is where the points are.

Learning objectives

By completing this homework, you will:

  1. Read OLS and logit coefficients in business terms: say what a coefficient means for a decision, not just whether it is positive and significant.
  2. Spot when two variables are related by construction, because one is part of the other, and why such a regression tells you nothing.
  3. Separate what the studio knows before a film is made from what it learns after, and see why a better-fitting model can be the wrong one to decide with.
  4. Turn a model into a decision: pick a probability threshold, and recommend a slate with specific numbers.

What the file contains

Each row is one film. The file contains the 200 highest-grossing films of each year from 2000 to 2024. Keep this in mind: every film in this list did well. There are no failures in it.

Column Type Description
title text Film title.
year integer Year of release, 2000 to 2024.
rank_in_year integer Worldwide box office rank within its release year, 1 to 200.
worldwide number Total worldwide box office, in US dollars.
domestic number Box office in the United States and Canada, in US dollars.
foreign number Box office everywhere else, in US dollars.
rating number Average audience rating, 1 to 10. Missing for some films.
vote_count integer Number of users who rated the film.
language text Original language, as a two-letter code (en is English).
genres text Genres for the film, separated by commas. Missing for some films.
production_countries text Countries involved in production, separated by commas.

Required cleaning and new variables

Report the number of rows, and how many films are missing rating, genres, and language. Two sentences.

Then apply two rules, and say in one sentence how many films each one removes:

  • Drop films with a missing rating, genres, or language. Every regression below uses these variables, and R would drop these films silently anyway; dropping them yourself keeps the same films in every model.
  • Drop films with zero votes (vote_count equal to 0). Q3 takes the log of vote_count, and the log of zero does not exist: R turns it into minus infinity and lm() stops with an error.

Then create these five variables. You will use them throughout:

Variable How to build it
foreign_share foreign divided by worldwide. A number between 0 and 1. This is your main outcome.
foreign_dominant 1 if foreign is greater than domestic, 0 otherwise.
first_genre The first genre listed for each film. Keep the six most common as their own categories and group everything else as Other.
english 1 if language is en, 0 otherwise.
log_domestic The natural log of domestic.

Q1. Two checks before any modeling

Before you model anything, you need to know two things about this data.

Check 1: how the three revenue columns fit together.

For each film, check whether domestic plus foreign equals worldwide. How many films pass?

Then answer: if worldwide is just domestic plus foreign, what would a regression of worldwide on domestic tell you? Hint: one is part of the other. Two or three sentences. This is why the rest of this homework uses foreign_share instead.

Check 2: the films with no US release.

Some films in this data have domestic equal to zero. These are films that never opened in US theaters.

  1. Report how many films have domestic equal to zero.
  2. Among those films, what share is foreign_dominant? Why is that share not a surprise?
  3. Report the share of all films that are foreign_dominant, then the share among films with domestic above zero. The two numbers are not close.
  4. Drop these films for the rest of the assignment, and give one reason why keeping them would be a problem. Two hints: what is foreign_share for every one of them, and what is log_domestic?

Use this sample for the rest of the assignment. Report how many films are in it.

Q2. Which genres travel

Run this regression:

foreign_share ~ first_genre + english + year

Use Comedy as your baseline genre. It is one of the two most common genres in the data, which makes it a useful comparison point.

Answer three questions:

  1. Which genres earn a larger share of their money abroad than Comedy does, and which earn less? Report the two or three largest coefficients in percentage points.
  2. How large is the english coefficient compared to the genre coefficients? What does that tell you about whether language or genre matters more here?
  3. What does the coefficient on year say about the direction the international market has moved since 2000? Multiply it by 25 to get the change across the whole period. Is that change large enough to matter for a slate being decided today?

Q3. Two models, and which one you can actually use

This is the most important question in this homework.

Run the Q2 regression again, with three variables added:

foreign_share ~ first_genre + english + year + rating + log(vote_count) + log_domestic

Put both models side by side in one table. The second model has a higher R-squared.

Now answer four questions:

  1. Go through the three new variables one at a time. For each one, say whether the studio would know that number before deciding to make the film. A film that has not been made yet has no audience rating and no vote count.

  2. Look again at how you built foreign_share. It is foreign / worldwide, and worldwide includes domestic. So domestic is on both sides: as a predictor, and inside the outcome. What does that do to the coefficient on log_domestic? Can you read it as “films that earn more at home travel less”? Two or three sentences.

  3. Compare the genre coefficients across the two models. Pick the genre with the largest coefficient in the first model and report its value in both models. Does the second model make that genre’s advantage look larger or smaller?

  4. Which model will you use for your recommendation? A higher R-squared does not make a model more useful for a decision. Say which model you are using and why, in two or three sentences.

Q4. From a probability to a decision

Run this logistic regression, using only the variables from Q2:

foreign_dominant ~ first_genre + english + year

Report odds ratios as well as coefficients. Say which variables raise and which lower the odds that a film earns more abroad than at home.

Then answer two questions:

  1. Is the model better than the obvious alternative? Suppose you ignored the model and predicted the more common outcome for every single film. How often would you be right? Now report how often your model is right, using a 0.5 cutoff. Report both numbers. Is the model worth having?

  2. Pick a threshold. The studio will pay for a full international marketing campaign for some films but not all. Above what predicted probability would you pay for one? Give a number between 0 and 1.

    Justify it by what each mistake costs. Spending on a film that does not travel wastes money. Not supporting a film that would have travelled loses revenue. Say which mistake worries you more and why your threshold reflects that. There is no correct answer here, but there are answers you can defend and answers you cannot.

Q5. The slate

Using the model you chose in Q3, recommend how many of the 10 films should be in each genre.

Give slot counts that add to 10. A direction such as “more animation” is not an answer. No genre may get more than 4 slots: the studio will not bet the year on one kind of film.

Before you decide, compute the correlation between log_domestic and foreign_share. Report the number and say what it means in one sentence. Then look at the average domestic revenue by first_genre: do the genres that travel best earn less at home, or more?

Then write 4–5 sentences explaining your slate. Name the genre you are giving up slots on, and say what you are losing by doing that.

Interpretation guidelines

For every regression:

  1. Use business units. “Animation earns about 21 percentage points more of its revenue abroad than Comedy” is an interpretation. “The coefficient is positive and significant” is not.
  2. Name the trade-off. Every recommendation costs something. Say what yours costs.
  3. Stay inside your evidence. This data has box office, ratings, votes, language and genre. It has no production budget, no marketing spend, no streaming revenue, and no films outside the top 200 of their year. You may make assumptions, but write them down.

Write for a manager who understands the business but does not program.
